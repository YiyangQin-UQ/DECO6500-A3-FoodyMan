<a id="prototype-scope"></a>
## Prototype mechanism and scope — PDF p. 17 (printed p. 14)

The prototype makes a narrow responsibility handoff visible: a housemate reports long-stored food, the report appears on the household board, and another relevant housemate can accept responsibility and mark the issue done. The finder is not automatically assigned the handling work. Accepting a task is distinct from owning the food.

The report states:

> Reporting does not automatically assign the work to the finder.

The prototype does not identify or verify the owner, or send a targeted reminder. It relies on shared visibility and voluntary acceptance of responsibility. A household agreement includes a limited three-day clearing rule, accepted voluntarily. The report lists no sanction, enforcement, dispute resolution, forced assignment or escalation when nobody acts. It also distinguishes an interface task being completed from physical food being used or discarded.

The intervention starts after someone notices an item. It is not a complete inventory, freshness detector or food-waste measurement system. Food-waste reduction remains a possible downstream consequence of clearer responsibility and subsequent action.

<a id="evaluation-coordination"></a>
## Coordination work — PDF p. 73 (printed p. 70), Section 11.4

The A2 evaluation asked whether the handoff could fit household fridge practices without excessive coordination or maintenance work. Among 15 moderated participants, extra setup steps were recorded for 9, setup assistance for 8, and anticipated difficulty maintaining setup for 3. After the Share condition, 3 of 12 valid records indicated further confirmation and 9 did not.

Separately, 17 paired responsibility-scenario ratings recorded lower confirmation need for 12 people and unchanged ratings for 5. Those ratings were not a controlled Share comparison or a count of observed contact. Qualitative analysis covered six coded cases. Taken together, the materials described potential fit alongside checking, updating and maintenance work. Sustainable total coordination effort was not measured.

<a id="evaluation-hesitation"></a>
## Confidence and hesitation — PDF p. 74 (printed p. 71), Section 11.5

The 17 paired U/E responses showed lower reported hesitation for 10 people and lower confirmation need for 12. Confidence increased for 8, was unchanged for 6 and decreased for 3. Fifteen people completed moderated sessions; two contributed prototype-based questionnaire responses without a moderated follow-up interview. Six cases entered the qualitative synthesis.

The report interprets the results as an association between explicit responsibility and lower stated hesitation or confirmation needs in those scenarios, with mixed confidence. U preceded E and the scenario information changed. Share and personally claiming responsibility were not isolated. The material therefore provides the A3 starting point: an interface state can help clarify a handoff while household coordination and authority remain open questions.

## Systemic Question Register — PDF pp. 84–88 (printed pp. 81–85), Section 14

The register records questions that depend on household rules, relationships, continuing work and wider outcomes. It distinguishes these questions from interface fixes and weaknesses in the earlier evaluation.

| Entry | Question recorded in A2 | A3 relevance |
|---|---|---|
| R-01 | Who responds when nobody accepts responsibility for a reported item? | Reporting starts a handoff; voluntary acceptance can still leave it unanswered. |
| R-02 | Would sustained adoption reduce consumer-level food waste? | Food waste is a downstream outcome; resolving an app report does not establish waste prevention. |
| R-03 | Who carries the maintenance work of a shared food record over time? | Setup, checking and repeated updating may fall unevenly on housemates. |
| R-04 | Who has authority to grant use of food, and how much of it? | A Share state does not settle the social scope of permission or jointly held rights. |
| R-05 | What does a persistent record of reporting and non-response do to household relationships? | Visible records may affect accountability, fairness and communication. |
| R-06 | How do trust and shared norms shape collective use of the handoff? | Existing trust, conflict and responsibility norms can shape adoption and continued coordination. |

R-01 and R-02 were carried forward from the original framing; R-03 to R-06 were added from the evaluation. The handover to A3 points from the single interaction toward the household: who responds, who does the work, whose authority a permission expresses, and how relationships affect these decisions. The register ranked R-06 as its most important unresolved question at that point. These register entries are questions for later inquiry, rather than established findings about households.

<a id="author-contribution"></a>
## A3 author's role in A2 — PDF p. 94 (printed p. 91), Section 17.1

The A3 author implemented and iterated the FoodyMan web prototype, later prepared quantitative data and summaries, and consolidated the team's knowledge record. The design decision was to let a finder flag a problem without automatically becoming responsible for handling it. Ownership, permission and responsibility were kept distinct. Feedback led to adding Share and positioning it alongside Needs Attention before the evaluation version was frozen.

The author's reflection identifies unanswered reports as an unresolved boundary of the narrow prototype. The report also records the author's dependence on the team's evidence plan and testing protocol, and distinguishes numerical recomputation from independent human review. This contribution explains the A3 author's position as both the prototype's designer and the evaluator of its wider decision conditions.
